In *Apocalypse Now* (1979), Captain Benjamin Willard is sent up a river into Cambodia to find Colonel Kurtz and "terminate his command". The journey is long, hot, wet and violent, and Willard is a professional: a special operations officer who has been in the war too long to care about anything that does not work.

That is the brief for this episode of **Recast**, the series where we choose the watch we would give a character today. Not three watches this time, but **one**: a single tool watch that could follow Willard from the hotel room in Saigon to Kurtz's compound.

## The watch Willard actually wears: the Seiko 6105

Before recasting, credit where it is due. In the film, Martin Sheen wears a **Seiko 6105**, a 1970s automatic dive watch with a distinctive cushion case. It was a popular, affordable and reliable watch among soldiers of the period, and the film made it so famous that collectors now simply call it the **"Captain Willard"**. Seiko even reissued it with the SLA033.

It is a perfect choice for 1979. Our question is different: what would Willard wear if he were sent upriver today?

We chose the **Marathon Navigator SSNAV-D**, the stainless steel version of Marathon's pilot's watch. Three reasons:

1. **It is a real military watch.** Not a "military-style" design, but a watch from a brand that equips armed forces today.
2. **It does one job, everywhere.** Day, night, river, jungle: it is readable, tough and accurate, with nothing superfluous.
3. **It fits the character.** Willard does not care about status. He cares about the mission. So does this watch.

## Marathon: a family company that equips armies

**Marathon** has roots that go back to 1904 and the Wein family, and the company was founded in Montreal, Canada, in 1939. It is still family owned today, now in its fourth generation. Its watches are designed in Canada and made in Switzerland.

Marathon's history is military from the start: during the Second World War it supplied timepieces to the Allied forces, and today it equips the **US and Canadian armed forces**. Its watches are built to **MIL-PRF-46374**, the US government specification for military wristwatches, which sets requirements for legibility, durability and resistance.

The **Navigator** was born in 1986, developed with Kelly Air Force Base as a pilot's watch for the US Air Force. The SSNAV-D is its modern steel version, with a date.

On Willard's wrist, every line of that table has a purpose. Quartz means no winding and no worries about shocks. Sapphire resists scratches. The screw-down crown keeps the river out. And the 41mm steel case is big enough to read at a glance, small enough to disappear under a sleeve.

## What you can learn from this watch: tritium and the "H3" on the dial

The most useful detail on the Navigator is one you only fully appreciate at night. Look at the dial and you will see a small **"H3"** symbol. It stands for **tritium**, the radioactive isotope of hydrogen, and it explains why military watches glow in a different way from ordinary ones.

Most watches use **luminescent paint**: it absorbs light during the day and releases it in the dark, fading after a few hours. Tritium watches work differently. Each marker is a tiny sealed **glass tube** filled with tritium gas and coated inside with phosphor. The tritium continuously emits low-energy electrons that hit the coating and make it glow. No sunlight, no charging: the watch is readable all night, every night.

There is a trade-off. Tritium has a **half-life of about 12.3 years**, so the glow slowly gets weaker over time. For a soldier on a night patrol, or for Willard on a dark river, that is a small price for a dial you can always read. The radiation is safely contained in the sealed tubes.

## The second lesson: the 12-hour bezel

Unlike a diver's bezel, which counts minutes, the Navigator's bezel is marked with **12 hours** and turns in both directions. It has two simple uses:

- **A second time zone.** Rotate the bezel by the time difference: the hour hand now shows local time on the dial and the second time zone on the bezel. Useful for coordinating with a command that runs on another clock.
- **Elapsed hours.** Align the bezel's triangle with the hour hand at the start of a long task, and you can read how many hours have passed at a glance.

For a mission that lasts days and depends on radio contact with headquarters, that is exactly the right tool.

## Why the Navigator is the right recast

The Seiko 6105 was the watch of its moment: affordable, tough and everywhere among the troops in the 1970s. The Marathon Navigator plays the same role today. It is not a luxury object pretending to be a tool, but a tool that happens to look good.

That is the kind of watch Willard would choose. Quiet, reliable, and still working when everything else around him has fallen apart.

Most of the watches we cover on Cult & Crown come from Bond films, war movies or 1980s classics. **Lucy in the Sky** is different: a quiet, strange film about an astronaut who cannot come back down to Earth. And on her wrist is one of the most interesting space watches ever made, the **Omega Speedmaster X-33**.

## The film: Lucy in the Sky (2019)

*Lucy in the Sky* is the first feature film directed by **Noah Hawley**, the creator of the TV series *Fargo* and *Legion*. Released in October 2019 by Fox Searchlight, it stars **Natalie Portman** as **Lucy Cola**, a NASA astronaut who returns from a mission in space and slowly loses her grip on ordinary life, alongside Jon Hamm, Zazie Beetz, Dan Stevens and Ellen Burstyn.

The story is loosely inspired by the case of NASA astronaut Lisa Nowak, although the film takes many liberties. It was not a success: critics were harsh and it earned less than half a million dollars worldwide. But almost every review praised Portman's performance, and the film's look, with its shifting aspect ratios and space sequences, is worth a watch.

## The watch: Omega Speedmaster X-33

On Earth, Lucy wears an **Omega Speedmaster X-33** on a titanium bracelet. According to Christopher Ward's Loupe magazine, which looked at Natalie Portman's watches on and off screen, it is a 1998 X-33: a modern take on the Moonwatch with a quartz movement and a titanium case.

It is a perfect choice for the character. Lucy is an astronaut of the Space Shuttle era, not of Apollo, and the X-33 is exactly the watch of that period: technical, functional and made for people who actually fly.

## The story of the X-33

The **Speedmaster X-33** was unveiled on **28 March 1998** at NASA's **Johnson Space Center** in Houston. The idea came from **General Tom Stafford**, an Apollo astronaut who sat on Omega's board, and it was developed with feedback from astronauts and pilots. It was never meant to replace the mechanical Moonwatch, but to complement it with functions a hand-wound chronograph cannot offer.

What makes it special:

- a **titanium** case, much lighter than steel;
- **analog hands** for a quick read of the time, combined with a **digital display** for everything else;
- a **mission timer**, a second time zone in **UTC**, a 1/100-second chronograph and an **80 dB alarm**, loud enough to hear inside a noisy cockpit;
- a quartz movement, accurate and independent from winding.

The caseback carries the engraving **"Flight-Qualified by NASA for Space Missions"**, and the X-33 is still on astronauts' wrists today. According to Luxury Bazaar, the whole **Artemis II** crew wore second-generation X-33s in the days before their 2026 launch.

## What you can learn from this watch: why astronauts wear analog and digital

The X-33 answers a question every space watch has to solve: what should a watch do that a computer cannot?

**Analog hands** are the fastest way for the human eye to read time. You do not need to read numbers, you just see the angle, even with a quick glance in a moving cockpit. **Digital displays**, on the other hand, are precise: they can show seconds, a second time zone or a countdown to the exact moment.

In space, both matter. Missions are coordinated in **UTC**, the universal time used by mission control, while astronauts still need to know the local time at home. And the **mission elapsed time**, the time passed since launch, is the clock everything on board is measured against. The X-33 puts all three on the wrist, with an alarm loud enough to wake you on a spacecraft. That is why, decades after the mechanical Moonwatch, astronauts still wear it.

## The X-33 today

The original X-33 was discontinued in 2006, and Omega later replaced it with the **Speedmaster Skywalker X-33**, developed with the European Space Agency, and then the **X-33 Marstimer**. The early generations are now found on the second-hand market and have become a cult watch among Speedmaster collectors: less famous than the Moonwatch, but closer to how astronauts actually work today.

## Why this watch matters

*Lucy in the Sky* is a film about the distance between space and ordinary life. The X-33 sits exactly on that line: a professional instrument built for orbit, worn by Lucy at home, in the office, in the car. It reminds her, and us, of where she has been.
